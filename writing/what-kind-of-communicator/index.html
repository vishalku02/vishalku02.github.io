What Kind of Communicator Does AI Ask Me to Become?

A Reflective Analysis on Authoring Explorable Explanations with Generative AI

Vishal Kumar

Author’s note. This paper was written by Vishal Kumar in collaboration with Claude Opus 4.7 (Anthropic), used as a thinking and drafting partner throughout. All ideas, evidence selections, and arguments are mine; Claude helped me organize, articulate, and prose-edit them. The full conversation log is available at [link]. I want this disclosed up front because the paper’s own argument concerns honest communicative practice with AI, and concealing that practice would undercut the position I am defending.

Introduction

Over three studio sessions across weeks five, seven, and eight, my collaborators and I used Claude Code to build three explorable explanations: a coral bleaching simulation for seventh graders in Alaska (with Felicia Yan, Manuel Cardenas, and Rafael Bitanga), a pasta-misconception game in the style of Overcooked and Cooking Mama (with Brooke Ho, David Chang, and Saad Ashraf), and a sonified gravity simulation in which cartoon characters bungee-jump on different planets (with Ishita Kumar and Tonya Murray). This paper is a reflective analysis of those sessions — not of the artifacts themselves, but of what happened in the prompting. Drawing on the Gulf of Envisioning framework from Subramonyam, Pea, and colleagues (2024) and the task-level abstraction model proposed in SimStep (Kaputa, Subramonyam, et al., 2026), I diagnose the envisioning gaps that surfaced in my own prompt logs (Section 1), trace where design agency lived across my process (Section 2), and articulate a position on what designing with AI asked me to become as a communicator (Section 3). The argument that emerges is that the Gulf of Envisioning is less a usability problem to engineer around than a diagnosis of our own communicative literacy — and that the practice of bridging it, repeated, is itself a form of learning that compounds.

Section 1: Diagnosing My Own Envisioning Challenges

Across three studio sessions — a coral bleaching explorable with Rafael, a pasta-misconception game, and a sonified gravity simulation with Ishita Kumar and Tonya Murray — I prompted Claude Code as a building collaborator. The Gulf of Envisioning framework from Subramonyam, Pea, and colleagues (2024) gives me a vocabulary for what was happening in those exchanges that I didn’t have at the time. Below I trace four moments that illustrate three of the sub-gaps the paper names — task-output, instruction, capability — plus a fourth I want to call intent articulation, where the gulf isn’t between prompt and output but between my own fuzzy intent and the prompt I committed to.

The dinner table that never was (intent articulation). In the coral session, I prompted: “we want to start off with the scene of a dinner plate with salmon on it.” When I reflected on this session weeks later, I remembered wanting “a whole family at the dinner table” and being disappointed that the AI gave me only a plate of fish. But the log shows the AI rendered exactly what I asked for. The family was never in the prompt. This is intent articulation in its purest form — the kind of gap Subramonyam, Pea, and colleagues describe when designers’ goals are not yet “well-formed” before prompting begins. Prompting forced a premature commitment, and the commitment overwrote the original intent so completely that I forgot I had ever made it. No re-prompting strategy could have bridged this, because at the moment of writing I didn’t know I was leaving something out.

Fragment prompting (instruction gap). Later in the same session: “no keep the bridge metaphorical, reefs as a parable as an indicator for what’s happening to your local waters.” I prompted in note-to-self fragments throughout coral — no subject, internal references, sentences that finish thoughts I never started out loud. I was treating Claude as a collaborator who already shared my context. Subramonyam, Pea, and colleagues frame the instruction gap as uncertainty about what kind of language and framing the model responds to; my version of it was almost the inverse — a kind of over-trust in social-communicative continuity. The AI did sometimes bridge these fragments. But the bridging was its inference, not my articulation, which means the design decisions encoded in those moments are not fully mine.

Cooking mama, post-hoc (capability gap). After Claude’s first build of the pasta game, I voice-noted a critique: “one, it would be cool if, like, you were a player and you could, like, move around to the different ingredient stations. Um, two, moving to those stations to trigger, like, cooking mama, like, mini games…” My original prompt asked for none of this. The walking player, the station mini-games, the customer sprites — these only became askable after I saw what the first version wasn’t. This is the capability gap the authors describe as not knowing what the model can do well enough to formulate the request. My strategy here was iterative — I bridged the gap by using the first output as a scaffold for imagining the second. But it also means the simulation’s mechanical ambition was set by Claude’s first attempt as much as by my initial vision.

Bungee in space (intent articulation, again, at three levels at once). In the gravity session, I prompted: “I would like five different [kinds] of the explosion sounds. […] also make the falling noise a whistle type of noise. bungee jumping in space.” This single prompt operates at three levels of abstraction: implementation (five tiers of synthesis), task (a whistle), and vibe (“bungee jumping in space”). The last is the most evocative line in any of my logs and also the most under-specified — and the AI translated it into damped spring oscillations, a starfield, and a striped cord, without any of those choices having been mine. I accepted them readily because they felt right; but my scratchpad reflection — that I cannot fully explain why my most recent simulation works the way it does — finds its evidence here. Looking across the gravity session, I notice something my memory had not surfaced: I prompted almost entirely at the implementation level. There is no prompt that names a pedagogical concept (force, gravitational acceleration, the felt sense of mass). Compare this to coral, where I explicitly listed three learning hypotheses before any building. The intent articulation gap is visible in the gravity logs not as fuzzy prompts but as a missing kind of prompt — I was prompting around the concept without ever stating it.

Section 2: Tracing Design Agency in My Process

If Section 1 names the gaps, Section 2 asks who was actually deciding what when those gaps were operating. Looking back at the same logs through this lens reveals a pattern I didn’t see while building: my design agency was not constant across the sessions or even within them. It moved depending on the level of abstraction I was prompting at, and on whether Claude surfaced its decisions back to me as choices.

Highest agency: when the AI surfaced its decisions as questions. The clearest moment of authorship in the pasta session came not from a prompt I wrote but from a question Claude asked back. After the initial design sketch, Claude returned: “Two design calls I want your input on: Should salt in the kitchen mini-game have zero effect on time, or a barely-visible one? […] Failure mode: time pressure or calmer?” My response was substantive: “a barely visible one, like we need to draw attention ot the fact that not much changes, right? maybe the time shakes a bit on the screen and it’s really just a sliver. and we have time pressure. so users might want to add more salt ot make that timer go down, but they’ll pour too much, ruin the saltiness, and see the timer won’t budge that must.” That single reply contains the pedagogical heart of the explorable — the misconception bait, the visible-but-tiny feedback, the time pressure that motivates the trap. This is what Kaputa, Subramonyam, and colleagues (2026) describe as task-level abstraction in SimStep: the human declares what should happen pedagogically, the system handles the rest. Crucially, it happened inside open-ended prompting — not because the framework forced it, but because Claude offered me a discrete choice. My agency was high here because the decision was made legible before being made.

Lowest agency: implementation prompts with implicit pedagogical drift. The gravity session was the opposite. Across nearly every prompt, I worked at the implementation level: “use different character voices for different planets”, “five different their of the explosion sounds”, “could we attach characters to the different voice dictation pitches.” These are feature requests, not pedagogical decisions, but each one quietly committed the simulation to a particular pedagogical stance — that gravity should be felt sonically rather than calculated, that personality and character matter more than the equation F = m × g, that play comes before formalization. None of those commitments were prompts I wrote. They were the cumulative effect of prompting at the wrong level. I felt least in control here precisely because I never felt out of control — the simulation grew under my hands and looked like mine, but its theory of how learners should encounter gravity was Claude’s by default.

The bungee line as a hinge. Between these two extremes is “bungee jumping in space.” That prompt accomplished, in five words, what most of my pasta prompts struggled to accomplish in paragraphs — it gave Claude a coherent design direction that propagated through dozens of downstream choices (damped oscillations, starfield, striped cord, character sprites). But the cost is that I cannot inventory those choices now. I authored the vibe; Claude authored everything below it. The vibe-level prompt feels like maximum agency — one line shaped the whole scene — but it is closer to abdication. I gave up the right to decide each detail in exchange for coherence. Whether that is a fair trade depends on whether the details matter for learning, and in a simulation about gravity, they almost certainly do.

My process compared to SimStep. SimStep proposes a structured collaboration where the human commits to pedagogical goals and learner tasks at a deliberate level of abstraction, and the system handles representation and interaction below that line. My process was the inverse: I drifted across abstraction levels, sometimes naming pedagogical goals (coral’s three hypotheses), sometimes naming tasks (the salt feedback loop), sometimes naming only vibes (bungee in space), with no scaffold forcing me to clarify which I was doing. What I gained from open-ended prompting was the right to invite Claude into our community of practice as a participant who could surprise me — the pasta game’s mini-game stations, which I would never have specified, became the best part of the build. What I lost was the discipline SimStep’s abstraction would have imposed — a check at each level of decision-making that would have made the design choices mine to keep or refuse. Open-ended prompting gave me an artifact; SimStep would have given me an authored artifact, and the difference is whether I can stand behind every choice in it.

Section 3: A Position on Designing with AI

The position I want to argue is this: extending Roy Pea’s framework of distributed cognition (Pea, 1993) to account for AI requires us to treat the AI as a participant in the cognitive network whose quality of participation depends, materially, on the quality of our communication with it. The Gulf of Envisioning is not only a usability problem to be designed around; it is a diagnosis of our own communicative literacy. And the practice of bridging it is itself a form of learning that compounds.

The evidence from my own logs runs in both directions. When I prompted with discipline — coral’s three explicit learning hypotheses, or my detailed reply to Claude’s two design questions in the pasta session — the design choices were legibly mine. When I prompted at the wrong level of abstraction — the gravity session’s stream of implementation requests with no pedagogical anchor — Claude made choices I cannot fully account for, and the simulation became a thing I built but cannot stand behind in the SimStep sense. The gulf was not Claude’s failure to understand; it was my failure to be understood.

This reframing matters because it suggests a different question than the one usually asked of AI tools. The usual question is what can AI do? The question my logs push me toward is what kind of communicator does AI ask me to become? Subramonyam, Pea, and colleagues’ (2024) gulf framework, read alongside Pea’s (1993) distributed cognition, lets me name AI as a participant in a community of practice rather than an instrument acting on it. But participants in a community of practice are not equally well-served by all interlocutors. The communicative care — empathy, in the older and harder sense of the word, the discipline of imagining what someone else needs to be told — that we owe a human collaborator is the same care that produces good prompts. I am wary of anthropomorphizing AI; I am not claiming Claude has feelings to which empathy is owed. I am claiming that the practice of empathy — articulating fuzzy intent, naming what one means, checking whether one has been understood — is what good prompting requires, and is the same practice good human collaboration requires.

Here the math education concept of automaticity (Anderson, 1982) becomes useful. Repeated practice of articulation builds an automatic disposition toward it. The drill-in-practice of bridging the Gulf of Envisioning with Claude is, by the same mechanism, drill-in-practice of communicative care more broadly. The artifact gets better. The communicator does too. And the communicator carries that practice into every human collaboration that follows.

This is the abundance argument: prompting AI well is not a transaction with a tool. It is a rehearsal of the communicative practices a flourishing community of practice requires. Treating the robot well is, in the most practical sense available, a way of treating each other better.
